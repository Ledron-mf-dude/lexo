In an office, trust tends to grow almost by accident. You see a colleague arrive early, overhear them helping a client, share a joke by the coffee machine. Over months, these small signals add up to a sense that this person is competent and on your side. Remove the office, and most of those signals disappear. What remains is a name in a chat window and a status that switches between green and grey.

Not only does distance reduce the number of signals; it also distorts the ones that remain. A short reply that would sound perfectly neutral face to face can read as cold or irritated in writing. A colleague who takes a day to respond may simply be in another time zone, yet the silence is easily interpreted as indifference. Left unchecked, such misreadings accumulate, and teams begin to operate on suspicion rather than goodwill.

What the most effective remote teams seem to understand is that trust has to be built deliberately. The research on distributed work points to two distinct kinds. The first, sometimes called cognitive trust, rests on reliability: people do what they say they will do, when they say they will do it. The second, affective trust, is about believing that colleagues care about you as a person. Remote teams usually manage the first reasonably well, since deadlines and deliverables are visible. It is the second that tends to be neglected.

Building affective trust at a distance is less a matter of grand gestures than of small, consistent habits. Managers who open meetings with a few minutes of genuinely personal conversation, who explain the reasoning behind decisions rather than merely announcing them, and who admit their own mistakes openly, tend to lead teams in which people raise problems early. It is precisely this willingness to speak up that prevents small issues from becoming expensive ones.

None of this means that remote teams are doomed to be less cohesive than co-located ones. Some of the most tightly knit teams have never shared a room. But they did not get there by chance. They treated trust not as a by-product of working together, but as a piece of infrastructure — one that needs to be designed, maintained and, occasionally, repaired.

## Граматика
- inversion — *Not only does distance reduce* the number of signals; it also distorts… — Інверсія після *Not only* на початку: допоміжне дієслово перед підметом, як у питанні. Друга частина — *but / it also*.
- cleft-sentences — *It is the second that* tends to be neglected. … *It is precisely this willingness* to speak up *that* prevents… — Виділення через *It is … that*: наголошує, саме яка частина важлива.
- participle-clauses — *Left unchecked*, such misreadings accumulate. — Дієприкметниковий зворот замість *If they are left unchecked*: коротше й типово для письмової мови.
- relative-clauses-advanced — Managers *who* open meetings…, *who* explain…, and *who* admit… tend to lead teams *in which* people raise problems early. — Кілька означальних підрядних до одного іменника; *in which* — формальніше за *where*.
