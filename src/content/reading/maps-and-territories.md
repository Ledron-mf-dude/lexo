There is a short story, often retold, about an empire whose cartographers became so devoted to accuracy that they produced a map on the scale of one mile to the mile. It covered the entire country, matched it point for point, and was, for that very reason, entirely useless. The succeeding generations, less enamoured of mapmaking, abandoned it to the weather, and it survives only in tattered fragments in the desert.

The parable is usually invoked to make a deceptively simple point: a map is valuable precisely because of what it leaves out. A road map that also showed every tree, every change of soil and every postbox would be not more useful but less. To model something is to decide what does not matter, and the skill lies as much in the omission as in the inclusion.

The difficulty arises when we forget that we are holding a map at all. Economic models built on the assumption that people act rationally have proved extraordinarily productive; they have also, on occasion, been mistaken for descriptions of how people actually behave, with consequences that are well documented. A company's dashboard, distilling the activity of thousands of employees into a dozen figures, can become, for the managers who stare at it daily, the reality itself — so much so that improving the figures and improving the business come to be treated as one and the same. They are not, and the gap between them is where many of the costliest decisions are made.

Nor is the problem confined to institutions. Each of us navigates by internal maps: of our colleagues, of our cities, of our own abilities. These are necessarily partial, assembled from limited experience and updated far less often than the territory changes. The colleague we filed away as unreliable five years ago may long since have become the person most worth consulting; the shortcut we always take may now be the slowest route.

None of this argues for abandoning maps — we could not function for an hour without them. What it argues for is a particular habit of mind: holding one's models firmly enough to act on them and loosely enough to notice when the ground no longer matches. The cartographers in the story erred not in caring about accuracy, but in failing to ask what their map was for.

## Граматика
- noun-phrases-complex — *A company's dashboard, distilling the activity of thousands of employees into a dozen figures,* can become… the reality itself — Складна іменна група: ядро *dashboard* і дієприкметниковий зворот у комах, що його пояснює. Підмет довгий, присудок — *can become*.
- inversion-c2 — *Nor is the problem confined* to institutions. — *Nor* на початку речення вимагає інверсії: «І проблема не обмежується…».
- non-finite-c2 — *To model something is to decide* what does not matter. — Інфінітив як підмет і як іменна частина присудка: «Моделювати — означає вирішувати…». Книжна симетрична конструкція.
- modals-speculation — The colleague we filed away as unreliable five years ago *may long since have become* the person most worth consulting. — *May have* + V3 з *long since*: «можливо, давно вже став». Обережне припущення про зміну в минулому.
- cleft-advanced — the gap between them is *where* many of the costliest decisions *are made* — Виділення через *is where*: місце, де відбувається найважливіше.
