Hardly had the twentieth century taught towns to measure their success in traffic flow and retail floor space when a handful of them began to wonder whether they had been asking the wrong questions. The movement that grew out of that doubt — loosely known as "slow cities" — now counts a few hundred members across several continents, most of them places with fewer than fifty thousand inhabitants and a certain stubbornness about how life ought to be lived.

The principles are less a programme than a temperament. Member towns undertake to protect local produce and the small businesses that sell it, to favour pedestrians over cars in their historic centres, to curb noise and visual clutter, and to preserve the kind of public space in which people linger rather than pass through. Plastic signs give way to painted ones; the weekly market is treated not as a quaint survival but as civic infrastructure.

To its admirers, the movement represents a corrective to decades in which places came to resemble one another to the point of interchangeability: the same chains, the same ring roads, the same out-of-town shopping centres sucking life from the high street. A slow city, on this view, is simply one that has declined to become anywhere in particular — or rather, that insists on remaining somewhere.

Its critics are not short of objections. The model, they point out, is far easier to sustain in a picturesque hill town with a thriving tourist trade than in a struggling industrial one, where the priority is jobs rather than ambience. There is, moreover, an irony in the fact that the very qualities which make such towns pleasant to live in tend to make them expensive to live in. Not infrequently, the people best placed to enjoy the slow life are those who made their money in the fast one, while younger locals, priced out, commute in from the anonymous suburbs the movement defines itself against.

Such contradictions do not invalidate the idea, but they complicate it. Slowness, it turns out, is not merely a matter of pace; it is a question of who gets to set the pace, and for whom. Should the movement prove durable, it will be because it found an answer to that question — not because it hung better signs.

## Граматика
- inversion-c2 — *Hardly had the twentieth century taught* towns… *when* a handful of them began to wonder… — Інверсія після *Hardly* з Past Perfect і *when*: «Щойно…, як…». Не плутайте пари: *Hardly / Scarcely … when*, але *No sooner … than*.
- conditionals-c2 — *Should the movement prove* durable, it will be because… — Умовна інверсія з *should* замість *If the movement proves*: формально, з відтінком «якщо раптом».
- double-negatives — *Not infrequently*, the people best placed… — Літота: заперечення протилежного (*not infrequently* = «досить часто»). Пом'якшує твердження й додає іронії.
- cleft-advanced — it is a question of *who gets to set the pace, and for whom* — Підрядні-питання як іменна частина: зміщують акцент із «швидкості» на «владу над швидкістю».
- relative-clauses-advanced — the anonymous suburbs *the movement defines itself against* — Означальне підрядне без *which* і з прийменником у кінці: *defines itself against (them)*. Природніше, ніж *against which the movement defines itself*.
