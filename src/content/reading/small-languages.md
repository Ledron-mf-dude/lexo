Of the roughly seven thousand languages spoken today, around half are expected to fall silent by the end of the century. Most of them are spoken by small communities, often in remote areas, and in many cases the last fluent speakers are already elderly. Once the final speaker dies, a language may survive in recordings and dictionaries, but it is no longer anyone's way of seeing the world.

The loss is not merely sentimental. Languages encode knowledge that is easily overlooked. Some distinguish dozens of kinds of snow or sea ice, others classify plants according to their medicinal uses, and many contain place names that record events — floods, migrations, battles — of which no written history survives. When a language disappears, this knowledge rarely makes the transition into the dominant language intact.

The decline of a language is seldom the result of a single decision. More commonly, it follows a gradual shift: parents, wanting their children to succeed in school and at work, speak to them in the majority language. The children grow up understanding their grandparents' language but answering in another, and within a generation the chain of transmission is broken.

Not until relatively recently did revitalisation efforts begin to show what is possible. Hebrew, revived as an everyday language after centuries of mainly liturgical use, is the best-known case, but there are others. Welsh, once in steady decline, is now taught in every school in Wales and used in government, media and everyday life. Māori "language nests", in which preschool children are surrounded entirely by speakers, have produced a new generation of fluent speakers.

What these cases have in common is not a single method but a combination: institutional support, a place for the language in education, and — above all — the conviction among speakers themselves that the language belongs to the future and not only to the past.

## Граматика
- inversion — *Not until relatively recently did* revitalisation efforts *begin* to show what is possible. — Інверсія після *Not until…* на початку: допоміжне *did* перед підметом. Підкреслює, як пізно це сталося.
- nominalisation — *The decline of a language* is seldom *the result of a single decision*. — Номіналізація: процеси названо іменниками (*decline, result, decision*). Безособово й щільно.
- passive-advanced — around half *are expected to fall* silent by the end of the century — *Be expected to* — безособовий прогноз: «очікується, що…».
- participle-clauses — parents, *wanting* their children to succeed…, speak to them in the majority language — Дієприкметниковий зворот пояснює причину: = *because they want*.
- relative-clauses-advanced — events… *of which* no written history survives — Прийменник перед *which* у формальному стилі: «про які не збереглося жодної писемної історії».
