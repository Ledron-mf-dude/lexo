Nobody enjoys waiting in a queue, but not all waits are experienced equally. Ten minutes in one line can feel unbearable, while ten minutes in another pass almost unnoticed. Researchers who study waiting have found that our frustration depends far less on the actual time involved than on how that time is perceived.

One of the best-known findings is that unoccupied time feels longer than occupied time. This is why lifts often have mirrors beside them and why some theme parks entertain visitors who are standing in line. A frequently cited example concerns an airport whose passengers complained about waiting for their luggage. Rather than speeding up the baggage handlers, the airport moved the arrival gates further from the reclaim area. Passengers now spent most of the time walking, and the complaints largely stopped, even though the total time had barely changed.

Uncertainty makes waiting worse as well. A wait whose length is unknown feels longer than one that has been announced, which is why many services now display estimated waiting times — and why those estimates are often deliberately pessimistic. Being told that you will wait fifteen minutes and then being served after ten produces a small sense of relief; the reverse produces irritation, even though the wait was identical.

Perceived fairness matters too. People tend to tolerate a long wait in a single queue that feeds several counters far better than a shorter wait in parallel queues, where they can watch others who arrived later being served first. Few things provoke more anger than the impression that someone has jumped ahead.

Underlying all of this is a simple insight: waiting is not just a logistical problem but a psychological one. Organisations that grasp this can make customers considerably happier without making them wait any less.
