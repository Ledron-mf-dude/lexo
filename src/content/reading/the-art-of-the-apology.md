Few rituals of public life are performed as often, or as badly, as the apology. A company leaks its customers' data; a politician is recorded saying something unforgivable; a celebrity is caught out in a lie. Within hours, a statement appears, and within minutes of its appearance it is being dissected online for evasions. Rarely does it survive the scrutiny.

The reasons are not hard to find. Most public apologies are drafted with one eye on the injured party and the other on the legal department, and it is generally the second eye that prevails. Hence the familiar formulas: regret "if anyone was offended", sorrow that "mistakes were made", a commitment to "learning lessons" that are never specified. Each phrase is engineered to sound contrite while conceding as little as possible. The passive voice, in particular, does heroic work, allowing wrongs to occur without anyone having committed them.

What, then, distinguishes an apology that is accepted from one that merely inflames? Those who study the subject tend to converge on a handful of elements. The wrong must be named plainly, not gestured at. Responsibility must be owned by a person or an institution, rather than dispersed into circumstance. The harm done to others should be acknowledged in their terms, not the offender's. And there must be some offer of repair, or at least a credible account of what will be done differently — failing which, the words are just that.

Of these, it is the first that is most often fudged and the last that is most often missing. Were apologies judged solely on their wording, many would pass. It is when they are set against subsequent conduct that they collapse. An airline that apologises "unreservedly" for stranding thousands of passengers, then quietly makes compensation as difficult as possible to claim, has not apologised at all; it has issued a press release.

There is, perhaps, a lesson here that extends well beyond corporate communications. The private apologies we make to friends, partners and colleagues are subject to the same temptations: to explain before acknowledging, to qualify before committing, to apologise for the other person's feelings rather than our own actions. Be that as it may, a good apology remains, at heart, remarkably simple. It says what happened, says that it was wrong, and says what will change. Everything else is decoration — and the more there is of it, the less the apology tends to be believed.

## Граматика
- inversion-c2 — *Rarely does it survive* the scrutiny. — Інверсія після заперечного прислівника на початку (*Rarely, Seldom, Never*): допоміжне дієслово перед підметом. Підсилює й робить стиль книжним.
- conditionals-c2 — *Were apologies judged* solely on their wording, many would pass. — Умовна інверсія замість *If apologies were judged*: *if* зникає, *were* виходить наперед. Формально й стисло.
- formulaic-subjunctive — *Be that as it may*, a good apology remains… simple. — Застиглий вираз із subjunctive: «Хай там як». Форма *be* без узгодження — релікт, змінювати її не можна.
- cleft-advanced — *It is the first that* is most often fudged and *the last that* is most often missing. — Розщеплене речення з двома виділеними частинами для контрасту.
- passive-advanced — allowing wrongs to occur *without anyone having committed* them — Перфектний герундій із власним підметом (*anyone having committed*): іронія над пасивом, який «ховає» виконавця.
