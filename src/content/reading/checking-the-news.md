A friend forwards you a dramatic headline: a famous scientist has supposedly discovered that coffee doubles your risk of heart disease. The message has been shared thousands of times. Should you believe it? Before you pass it on, it's worth spending two minutes checking.

First, look at the source. If the story comes from a website you have never heard of, that doesn't automatically mean it's false, but it should make you more careful. Search for the same news on well-known sites. If something that dramatic were true, major newspapers would almost certainly be reporting it.

Second, read beyond the headline. Headlines are written to make us click, and they often exaggerate. The article itself might say something much less exciting — for example, that one small study found a slight link in a specific group of people. "Doubles your risk" and "might be connected in some cases" are very different claims.

Third, check the date. Old stories often go viral again years later, as if they were new. A report that was accurate in 2015 may have been corrected since then.

Fourth, notice how the story makes you feel. Fake news is usually designed to make us angry or scared, because strong emotions make us share without thinking. If a post makes you furious, that is exactly the moment to slow down.

None of this requires special skills. It does require a habit of pausing. The next time a shocking message appears in your chat, ask yourself: Who says so? What do they actually say? When was it written? It can't take more than a couple of minutes — and it might stop you from spreading something that isn't true.
