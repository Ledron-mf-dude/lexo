People who learn that I translate novels for a living tend to ask one of two questions. The first is whether I use software. The second, asked more quietly, is whether something is always lost. The answer to the first is "occasionally, for a rough draft of the dullest passages". The answer to the second is "yes — and so is something gained, if you're lucky".

What gets lost is rarely the meaning. Meaning, in the narrow sense, travels surprisingly well. What resists is everything that rides alongside it: rhythm, register, the faint echo of a phrase the original reader would have recognised from a children's rhyme or an old advertisement. A character in a Ukrainian novel I worked on last year greets a neighbour with a formula that, to any Ukrainian ear, instantly marks her as a village woman of a certain age. In English I could render the words, or I could render the effect. I could not render both.

Translators differ in how they resolve such dilemmas, and so they should. Some lean towards the source, keeping the reader aware that the book comes from elsewhere — a foreign word left standing, a syntax that feels slightly unfamiliar. Others aim for a text that reads as though it had been written in the target language in the first place. I find myself somewhere in between, and not consistently so: it depends on the book, the sentence, sometimes the day.

The gains are harder to describe but just as real. Occasionally English offers a word that fits a moment better than the original did — not because the author chose badly, but because the two languages carve up experience differently. When that happens, I feel a small, guilty pleasure, as if I had been given something I was not owed. Authors I work with are, on the whole, generous about this. One told me that she had learned things about her own novel from questions I had asked, ones she had never been asked before and had never needed to ask herself.

Whenever someone suggests that translation will soon be done entirely by machines, I neither argue nor agree. Much of it will be, and in many contexts that is no loss at all. But a novel is not a set of instructions. Its sentences are doing several things at once, and to translate it is to choose, again and again, which of those things to keep. That choosing, for now, remains stubbornly human.

## Граматика
- ellipsis-substitution — "yes — and *so is* something gained, if you're lucky" — Підстановка *so is*: «і дещо також здобувається». Уникає повтору *something is gained*.
- ellipsis-substitution — Translators differ in how they resolve such dilemmas, and *so they should*. — *So they should* — емфатичне «і правильно роблять». Підстановка замість повтору *they should differ*.
- unreal-past — a text that reads *as though it had been written* in the target language in the first place — *As though* + Past Perfect: нереальне порівняння щодо минулого («так, ніби його написали»).
- reference-cohesion — questions I had asked, *ones* she had never been asked before — *Ones* замість повтору *questions*: підтримує зв'язність тексту без повторів.
- modals-speculation — a phrase the original reader *would have recognised* from a children's rhyme — *Would have* + V3: передбачуване знання, яке читач оригіналу мав би, на відміну від англомовного.
